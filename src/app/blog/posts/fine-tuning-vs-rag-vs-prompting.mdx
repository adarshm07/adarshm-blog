A general-purpose model doesn't know your company's documents, your
product's house style, or the exact format your downstream code expects.
There are three ways to close that gap, and teams often reach for the most
expensive one first.

- **Prompting** — change what you *ask*: instructions, examples, context.
- **RAG** (retrieval-augmented generation) — change what the model can
  *see*: fetch relevant documents and include them in the prompt.
- **Fine-tuning** — change the *model*: train it further on your own
  examples so its weights shift.

## Prompting: always start here

Prompting is underrated because it's cheap. Clear instructions, a few good
examples of input and expected output, and the relevant context in the
prompt solve a surprising share of problems — and you can iterate in
minutes.

Modern models have [large context windows](/blog/context-windows-and-caching),
so "the relevant context" can be a lot: a whole policy document, a long
style guide, dozens of examples. With prompt caching, a long, stable prefix
costs a fraction of normal price on repeat requests.

**Reach for something else when:** the knowledge is far too large for the
prompt, changes constantly, or you've measured that prompting has hit a
ceiling.

### What "good prompting" actually means

Before concluding that prompting has hit a ceiling, check that you've
tried the techniques that close most gaps:

- **Examples beat descriptions.** Three input/output pairs showing the
  exact format and tone usually do more than a paragraph describing them.
  Include a tricky case, not just easy ones.
- **Say what to do when unsure.** "If the ticket doesn't fit any category,
  use `other` and explain why" prevents confident guessing.
- **Structured output for machine consumers.** A schema removes a whole
  class of formatting failures — see
  [structured outputs](/blog/structured-outputs-from-llms).
- **Decompose.** One prompt that classifies, extracts and drafts a reply is
  harder to get right than three small prompts chained in code, each with
  its own eval.
- **Give it room to think** on hard tasks, and keep reasoning out of the
  final output when the consumer is code.

A prompt like this one, with its examples cached as part of a stable
prefix, is cheap to run at volume:

```
You classify support tickets. Categories: billing, bug, account, other.
Reply with JSON: {"category": ..., "confidence": "high" | "low"}.

Ticket: "I was charged twice for October."
{"category": "billing", "confidence": "high"}

Ticket: "The export button does nothing on Safari."
{"category": "bug", "confidence": "high"}

Ticket: "Can you delete my data and also why was I charged?"
{"category": "account", "confidence": "low"}

Ticket: "{{ticket}}"
```

Most teams that think they need fine-tuning for a classification task
haven't yet tried twenty good examples and an eval.

## RAG: when the knowledge is big or changing

[RAG](/blog/rag-retrieval-explained) splits your documents into chunks,
turns them into [embeddings](/blog/what-embeddings-actually-are), and at
question time retrieves the few chunks most relevant to the question and
puts them in the prompt.

It's the right tool for knowledge:

- **Large** — thousands of documents, far beyond any context window.
- **Changing** — update the index, and the next answer uses the new data.
- **Needing citations** — every retrieved chunk has a source to point to.
- **Permissioned** — retrieve only what this user is allowed to see.

Its weak spot is retrieval itself: if the right chunk isn't found, the
model can't use it. Most RAG quality work is chunking, search quality and
re-ranking — not the model.

## Fine-tuning: when you need a different behaviour

Fine-tuning trains the model further on many examples of inputs and ideal
outputs. It changes **how** the model responds — style, format, a narrow
skill — more than **what** it knows.

Good uses:

- **Making a small model good at one narrow task** — classification,
  extraction, routing — so it can replace a larger, slower, more expensive
  model for that job.
- **A very specific style or format** that must be consistent across huge
  volumes and that prompting can't pin down.
- **Shortening prompts** that would otherwise need many examples on every
  request.

The costs are real: you need hundreds to thousands of high-quality
examples, a training run, an evaluation set to prove it helped, and you
repeat all of it when you want to change the behaviour or move to a newer
base model.

### What the training data looks like

Fine-tuning data is a file of examples in the same chat format you use
for requests — typically JSON Lines, one conversation per line:

```json
{"messages": [{"role": "system", "content": "Classify the ticket."}, {"role": "user", "content": "I was charged twice for October."}, {"role": "assistant", "content": "{\"category\": \"billing\"}"}]}
{"messages": [{"role": "system", "content": "Classify the ticket."}, {"role": "user", "content": "Export does nothing on Safari."}, {"role": "assistant", "content": "{\"category\": \"bug\"}"}]}
```

The model learns to produce the assistant turns. That makes the quality
bar plain: every assistant turn is something you're teaching it to say.
Inconsistent labels, sloppy formatting or wrong answers in the training set
are learned as faithfully as the good ones. A few hundred carefully checked
examples usually beat thousands of noisy ones.

### Distillation: the most common good reason

The use case that pays off most reliably is **distillation**: a large,
capable model does the task well but is too slow or expensive at your
volume. So:

1. Prompt the large model carefully and validate its outputs on a
   representative sample with your eval set.
2. Run it over thousands of real inputs to generate training examples.
3. Fine-tune a much smaller model on those examples.
4. Evaluate the small model against the large one on held-out cases.

If the small model matches the large one on this narrow task, you get its
speed and price at the large model's quality — for this task only. That
"for this task only" is the trade: the small model has learned a narrow
skill, not general capability. Check your provider's terms on using one
model's outputs to train another.

### Lighter-weight tuning

You don't always have to update every weight. **Parameter-efficient**
methods such as LoRA train a small set of extra parameters on top of a
frozen base model. They're far cheaper to train and store — one base model
can serve many small adapters, one per task or customer — and are the
usual way open-weight models are tuned. Hosted fine-tuning services often
use similar techniques behind the scenes.

## Why fine-tuning is the wrong way to teach facts

It's the most common misconception. Fine-tuning on your documents does not
reliably make a model *know* them:

- Facts seen a handful of times in training get blurred, not memorised —
  the model learns to sound like your documents, which can make
  [hallucinations](/blog/why-llms-hallucinate) more convincing.
- Knowledge is frozen at training time; any update means retraining.
- There's no source to cite and no way to restrict who sees what.

For facts, retrieve them. For behaviour, consider tuning. A useful
shorthand: retrieval changes what the model is *looking at*; fine-tuning
changes what it *tends to do*. Teaching a fact by changing tendencies is
indirect at best.

## Questions that decide it

When a team is debating which approach to use, these questions usually
settle it faster than arguing about the techniques:

- **Is the gap knowledge or behaviour?** If the model gives wrong *facts*
  about your domain, it needs to see them: prompt or RAG. If it has the
  facts but answers in the wrong *way* — format, tone, structure — that's
  behaviour: prompting first, fine-tuning if prompting can't hold it.
- **How often does the knowledge change?** Weekly or faster rules out
  baking it into weights.
- **Do answers need sources?** Users, auditors or regulators asking "where
  did that come from?" points to retrieval.
- **Do different users see different data?** Permissions are enforced at
  retrieval time; a fine-tuned model can't forget what one user isn't
  allowed to know.
- **What's the volume and the latency budget?** High volume on a narrow
  task is where a distilled small model earns its keep.
- **Do you have an eval set?** If not, build it first. Without one, none of
  the three approaches can be shown to work, and fine-tuning in particular
  becomes an expensive way to change things you can't measure.

If the answers are "knowledge, changes often, needs sources", the decision
is made before anyone opens a training notebook.

## Long context or RAG?

Context windows are now large enough to hold entire manuals, which raises
a fair question: why retrieve at all?

- **Put it all in the prompt** when the corpus is small enough to fit
  comfortably, doesn't change often, and the questions need to see the
  whole thing — "compare section 4 with appendix B". With caching, a large
  stable prefix is affordable on repeat requests.
- **Retrieve** when the corpus is larger than the window, changes often,
  needs per-user permissions, or when cost and latency per request matter
  more than squeezing out the last bit of quality. Sending 200,000 tokens
  on every question is slower and pricier than sending the right 3,000,
  even with caching.

The two also combine: retrieve whole relevant documents rather than small
chunks, and let a long context hold them.

## Three scenarios

**A support assistant for a SaaS product.** The knowledge is the help
centre (hundreds of articles, edited weekly) plus each customer's account
state. *Prompting* sets tone and escalation rules; *RAG* over the help
centre supplies answers with citations; *tools* fetch account data live.
No fine-tuning — the knowledge changes too often, and the behaviour is easy
to describe.

**Classifying ten million documents a month.** A large model with a good
prompt hits 96% on the eval set but costs too much at that volume.
*Distil* it: generate labels with the large model, fine-tune a small one,
confirm it scores within a point on held-out data, and run the small model
through a batch API. Here fine-tuning is the right answer — after
prompting proved the task was learnable.

**Writing in a strict house style.** A legal team's drafts must follow a
template and phrasing conventions. Start with the style guide and examples
in a cached prompt. If evals show the model drifting over long documents
despite that, fine-tuning on a few hundred approved drafts is a reasonable
next step — while RAG still supplies the case facts.

## The maintenance bill

The upfront cost of each approach is visible; the ongoing cost is easy to
underestimate:

- **Prompts** need an eval set and a review when the model version changes.
- **RAG** needs an ingestion pipeline, re-indexing on document changes, and
  monitoring of retrieval quality.
- **Fine-tuned models** need the training data kept current, retraining
  when behaviour must change, and a full re-run of the whole process to
  move to each newer base model — or you stay on an ageing one while the
  general models around it improve.

That last point is easy to miss: base models keep getting better, and a
prompt or a RAG system usually benefits from an upgrade on day one. A
fine-tuned model has to be rebuilt to benefit.

## The order to try them

1. **Prompt.** Write clear instructions and examples; build a small
   [eval set](/blog/evals-for-llm-apps) so you can measure.
2. **Add retrieval** if the model lacks knowledge it needs.
3. **Fine-tune** only if evals show a behaviour gap that prompting and
   retrieval can't close — or to move a proven task onto a smaller, cheaper
   model.

They also combine: a fine-tuned model can still use RAG. But most teams
that skip straight to fine-tuning would have shipped sooner — and better —
with a good prompt and a retrieval step. The expensive option is sometimes the right
one; it should be the one you arrive at with evidence, not the one you
start from because it sounds the most like "real" machine learning.
