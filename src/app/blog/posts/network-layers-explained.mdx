"That layer, this layer" — the reason nobody remembers which is which is
that we're taught *seven* OSI layers as a memorization exercise, when the
internet actually runs on *four*. Let me untangle both, then get to the
part that matters in practice: the transport layer, where TCP and UDP make
two opposite bets on the same problem.

The core idea is **encapsulation**. Each layer wraps the layer above it in
its own envelope, does one job, and hands it down. Your data picks up a
transport header, then a network header, then a link header on the way
out — and each is peeled off in reverse on the way in. No layer needs to
understand the others; it just adds its stamp and passes the package along.

## The two models

The **OSI model** is the seven-layer textbook diagram. It's a reference,
not something anyone implements literally. The **TCP/IP model** is what the
internet actually uses — four layers that collapse OSI's top three into
one and its bottom two into one.

I'll walk them top to bottom — the direction your data travels when you
hit *send*.

## Application layer — HTTP, DNS, TLS

This is the only layer most of us touch directly. It's where protocols
speak in terms a human designed: `GET /index.html`, a DNS question for
`adarshm.com`, an SMTP `MAIL FROM`. OSI splits this into three (session,
presentation, application), but in practice your library — the HTTP
client, the TLS stack — handles all of it. The layer's defining trait: it
knows nothing about *how* the bytes get there, only what they say.

```text
GET /blog HTTP/1.1
Host: adarshm.com
Accept: text/html
```

That request is just a string. It has no idea it's about to be chopped
into segments, routed through a dozen networks, and reassembled.

## Transport layer — TCP and UDP

Here's the interesting layer, and the one your question is really about.
The network layer below can only do one thing: shove a packet toward a
host, best-effort, no promises. Packets can be dropped, duplicated,
reordered, or corrupted. The transport layer is where we decide **how much
we care** about fixing that — and it adds one more crucial thing: **ports**,
so the OS knows the packet is for your browser tab and not your mail
client.

Two protocols, two philosophies.

**UDP** is the honest minimalist. It adds ports and a checksum to a raw
packet and calls it done. No connection, no acknowledgements, no
retransmission, no ordering. A UDP header is 8 bytes.

```text
Source Port | Dest Port | Length | Checksum
   2 bytes  |  2 bytes  | 2 bytes|  2 bytes
```

Fire and forget. If a packet vanishes, UDP never knows and never tells
you. That sounds broken until you realize it's exactly right for some
jobs — more below.

**TCP** is the reliability machine. On top of the same unreliable network,
it manufactures the illusion of a clean, ordered, lossless byte stream.
That illusion costs a header of 20+ bytes and a fair amount of
bookkeeping:

- **Connection setup** — the three-way handshake (`SYN`, `SYN-ACK`,
  `ACK`) before any data flows, so both sides agree on starting sequence
  numbers.
- **Sequence numbers + ACKs** — every byte is numbered; the receiver
  acknowledges what it got, and anything unacknowledged is retransmitted.
  This is what makes delivery *reliable* and *in order*.
- **Flow control** — a sliding window so a fast sender can't drown a slow
  receiver.
- **Congestion control** — the sender backs off when the *network* shows
  signs of overload (this is why one lost packet can slow a whole
  stream).

The rule of thumb: **pick TCP when every byte must arrive; pick UDP when a
late byte is worse than a lost one.** In a video call, a packet that shows
up 300ms late is useless — you'd rather skip it and keep the conversation
live than stall the stream waiting for a retransmit. Live audio, gaming,
and DNS lookups all take that bet. Loading this blog post does not: a web
page with a missing chunk is broken, so HTTP rides on TCP.

Worth knowing: **QUIC** (the transport under HTTP/3) is built *on top of*
UDP, then re-implements TCP's reliability and ordering in user space —
getting TCP's guarantees while dodging its handshake and head-of-line
blocking. It's a neat reminder that these layers are conventions, not
laws.

## Internet layer — IP and routing

Below transport sits **IP**, whose one job is host-to-host delivery across
independent networks. It stamps each packet with a source and destination
**IP address** and hands it to the first router. Each router along the way
looks only at the destination, consults its routing table, and forwards
the packet one hop closer — no router knows the full path, and different
packets in the same conversation can take different routes.

IP is deliberately dumb and unreliable — no delivery guarantee, no
ordering. That's not a bug; it's the design. By pushing reliability *up*
to TCP and keeping the network layer simple, the internet stays cheap to
route and easy to scale. This is the famous **end-to-end principle**:
smarts live at the edges, the middle just forwards.

## Link layer — Ethernet, Wi-Fi

The bottom layer gets bits onto the actual medium — a copper cable, a
Wi-Fi channel, fiber. It deals in **MAC addresses** (hardware addresses
baked into your network card) and frames, and it only ever moves data
across a *single* physical hop: your laptop to your router, one router to
the next. Every hop re-wraps the packet in a fresh link-layer frame for
whatever medium it's crossing next. This is OSI's physical and data-link
layers fused into one.

## Putting it together

Load this page and the layers cooperate like an assembly line:

1. **Application** — your browser forms an HTTP `GET` for `/blog`.
2. **Transport** — TCP wraps it in a segment, tags the port, and (on a new
   connection) does its handshake.
3. **Internet** — IP wraps the segment in a packet addressed to the
   server's IP.
4. **Link** — Ethernet/Wi-Fi wraps the packet in a frame and puts it on
   the wire.

At the server, the same envelopes come off in reverse — link, internet,
transport, application — and the web server finally reads a plain HTTP
request, blissfully unaware of the journey. Each layer only ever talks to
its counterpart on the other side and trusts the layers below to do their
one job.

That's the whole mental model. When something breaks — a stalled download,
a laggy call, a site that won't resolve — knowing which layer owns which
job tells you where to look: DNS and TLS at the top, packet loss and
retransmits at transport, routing and reachability at the internet layer,
and cables and signal down at the link. Most debugging is just figuring
out which envelope didn't make it.
