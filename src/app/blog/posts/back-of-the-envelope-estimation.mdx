"Design a URL shortener" means nothing until you know how big it is. One
server and a SQLite file? A sharded cluster across three regions? Estimation
is how you find out in two minutes, before drawing boxes. You're after the
**order of magnitude**: 40 versus 4,000 versus 400,000 requests a second are
three different designs. 38.6 versus 40 doesn't matter.

## Numbers worth knowing

**Time.** A day is 86,400 seconds — call it **10<sup>5</sup>**. A month is
about 2.6 × 10<sup>6</sup> seconds, a year about 3 × 10<sup>7</sup>. So:

- 1 million per day ≈ **12 per second**
- 100 million per day ≈ **1,200 per second**
- 1 billion per month ≈ **400 per second**

**Sizes.** KB = 10<sup>3</sup>, MB = 10<sup>6</sup>, GB = 10<sup>9</sup>,
TB = 10<sup>12</sup> bytes. A typical row with a few short strings is
hundreds of bytes. A compressed photo is hundreds of KB. A minute of HD
video is tens of MB.

**Latency, roughly** — each line is about an order of magnitude apart:

**What one machine can do, very roughly**: a simple cached read endpoint
handles thousands to tens of thousands of requests a second; a single
relational database, thousands of simple writes a second; a cache node,
hundreds of thousands of operations a second. Real numbers depend heavily on
the workload — treat these as a sanity check, not a promise.

## Turn every number into a decision

An estimate is useful only if it changes the design. From the walkthrough:

- **40 writes a second** is nothing for one database. No write sharding is
  needed, and generating unique keys can be simple — a counter, or a random
  key with a collision check.
- **12,000 reads a second at peak**, mostly for popular links, says: put a
  cache in front, and serve redirects from the cache. Several stateless app
  servers behind a [load balancer](/blog/load-balancing-strategies) cover
  it with room to spare.
- **3 TB over five years** fits on one machine, but one machine is a single
  point of failure. Replicate for availability. Shard later, by key hash, if
  assumptions change — see
  [sharding and replication](/blog/database-sharding-and-replication).
- **35 GB of hot data** fits in memory on one cache node, or across a few.
- **3.5 trillion possible 7-character keys** against 6 billion needed means
  collisions are rare. Six characters (57 billion) would also work, but with
  less headroom.

Bandwidth is worth a line too: 4,000 reads a second × 500 bytes is 2 MB/s.
Negligible — and saying so is part of the answer.

The full design built on these numbers is in the
[URL shortener case study](/blog/design-a-url-shortener).

## Four more worked examples

The same method, on systems with very different shapes. Each one ends at
the number that matters most — it's rarely the same one twice.

### A chat app

50 million daily users, 40 messages each a day, 200 bytes a message.

- Messages: 50M × 40 = 2 × 10⁹/day → ÷ 10⁵ = **~20,000/s**, peak maybe 3×.
- Storage: 2 × 10⁹ × 200 B = 400 GB/day → **~150 TB/year**, ×3 for
  replication.
- Connections: if 40% are online at peak, **20 million open sockets**. At
  ~200,000 per gateway, that's about 100 gateways.

**The number that matters**: open connections. Message throughput is
moderate; holding 20 million long-lived sockets is what shapes the
architecture. See the [chat system](/blog/design-a-chat-system).

### Photo sharing

10 million uploads a day, 2 MB each after compression, three resized
copies adding another ~1 MB, 500 million feed views a day showing ~10
photos each.

- Upload storage: 10⁷ × 3 MB = 30 TB/day → **~11 PB/year**.
- Upload bandwidth: 30 TB ÷ 10⁵ s = **300 MB/s** inbound.
- Image views: 5 × 10⁹/day → **~50,000/s**; at ~200 KB a thumbnail-sized
  image, that's **10 GB/s** outbound.

**The number that matters**: 10 GB/s out. No origin should serve that;
it's a [CDN](/blog/cdn-explained) problem. And petabytes a year means
object storage with tiering — recent photos hot, old ones cold — not
servers with disks.

### Video streaming

1 million concurrent viewers at peak, average bitrate 5 Mbit/s.

- Egress: 10⁶ × 5 Mbit/s = 5 Tbit/s.
- Per edge server pushing ~40 Gbit/s: **~125 servers**' worth of capacity,
  spread across many locations near viewers.

**The number that matters**: 5 Tbit/s is beyond any single data centre's
comfortable egress, so the design is defined by edge caching and adaptive
bitrate, not by the application servers at all.

### A news feed

200 million daily users opening the feed 10 times a day; 50 million posts
a day; 200 followers on average.

- Reads: 2 × 10⁹/day → **~20,000/s**.
- Fan-out writes if you push each post to every follower: 50M × 200 =
  10¹⁰/day → **~100,000/s**.
- Feed storage at 800 IDs × 8 bytes per user: 2 × 10⁸ × 6.4 KB ≈ **1.3 TB**.

**The number that matters**: fan-out writes outnumber reads 5:1 — but each
is tiny and asynchronous, which is why push still wins. The tail (accounts
with millions of followers) is what breaks it; see the
[news feed](/blog/design-a-news-feed).

## From requests per second to servers

Once you have a peak rate, the server count is one division — if you know
what one server can do. Two ways to estimate that:

**From CPU time.** If a request takes 5 ms of CPU, one core handles ~200 a
second; a 16-core server, ~3,000. At 12,000 a second peak, that's 4
servers — plan 6–8 for headroom and failures.

**From concurrency, with Little's law.** The average number of requests
in flight equals arrival rate × time each spends in the system:

```
in-flight = throughput × latency
12,000 req/s × 0.05 s = 600 concurrent requests
```

Little's law is the most useful formula in capacity planning because it
connects three numbers you usually have two of. It tells you how many
database connections you need (rate × query time — see
[connection pooling](/blog/connection-pooling)), how many worker threads,
or how many messages sit in a queue.

**Then add headroom.** Run servers at 50–70% of measured capacity at
peak, not 100% — latency climbs steeply as utilisation approaches 100%,
because queues form. And add N+1 or N+2 so losing a machine (or a whole
zone) doesn't overload the rest.

Multiply them together and 3 TB of "data" can mean 15–20 TB of provisioned
disk. That's exactly the kind of gap that turns "fits on one machine" into
"doesn't", so do the multiplication out loud.

## Sizing a cache

"Put a cache in front" invites the next question: how big? Two inputs
decide it.

**The hot set.** Traffic is rarely uniform — a small fraction of items gets
most of the reads. A common working assumption is that ~20% of items get
~80% of traffic; for social content the skew is often far steeper. Cache
the hot fraction of *recent* data, not all of it:

```
URL shortener: 40 new links/s × 86,400 s × 500 B ≈ 1.7 GB of new links a day
keep the last ~20 days hot → ~35 GB
```

**The hit rate you need.** Work backwards from what the database can take.
If the database comfortably handles 2,000 reads a second and peak traffic
is 12,000, the cache must absorb at least 10,000 — an 83% hit rate, with a
margin on top. If your hot-set estimate can't plausibly deliver that, you
need read replicas as well as a cache.

Then check the cache's own limits: ~35 GB fits one node's memory, and
12,000 operations a second is a small fraction of what one cache node can
serve. One primary plus a replica, for availability rather than capacity.

## Estimating queues

For anything asynchronous — fan-out, emails, video transcoding — the
question is whether workers keep up, and how far behind they fall in a
burst.

- **Steady state**: workers must process faster than jobs arrive, with
  margin. If each job takes 200 ms and 600 arrive a second, you need at
  least 600 × 0.2 = 120 concurrent workers — Little's law again.
- **Bursts**: if a campaign enqueues 10 million jobs and workers drain
  2,000 a second, the backlog takes 5,000 seconds — about 80 minutes. Is
  that acceptable for this job type? If not, add workers, or prioritise
  so urgent jobs skip the line.
- **Queue storage**: 10 million jobs × 1 KB = 10 GB sitting in the broker.
  Fine for a log like Kafka; worth checking for an in-memory broker.

## An interview script

In an interview, estimation should take two or three minutes and leave a
small table on the whiteboard that the rest of the design refers back to.
A shape that works:

1. **Ask or state the scale**: daily active users, actions per user per
   day, read/write ratio, object sizes, retention. Write them down.
2. **Compute writes per second and reads per second**, average and peak.
3. **Compute storage** for the retention period, then mention the
   multipliers.
4. **Compute bandwidth** if large objects are involved; skip it with one
   sentence if they aren't.
5. **Say what each number implies**: "40 writes a second — one database";
   "12,000 reads a second — cache plus a few app servers"; "10 GB/s out —
   this is a CDN design".

Then move on. Returning to the table later ("we said 35 GB hot, so one
cache node") is what makes the estimates look like engineering rather than
ritual.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing bits and bytes.** Network speeds are in bits (Gbit/s), storage
  in bytes. A 1 Gbit/s link moves ~125 MB/s, not 1 GB/s. A factor of 8 is
  enough to change a design.
- **Using daily averages for capacity.** Traffic follows people's days:
  peak hours can be 3× the average, and launches or live events 10× or
  more.
- **Ignoring the read/write ratio.** "1,000 requests a second" hides
  whether that's 990 reads or 990 writes — two completely different
  systems.
- **Forgetting the fan-out.** One user action often becomes many internal
  requests: a page view might call ten services, and a post might write to
  a thousand feeds. Estimate the internal rate, not just the external one.
- **False precision.** "We need 37.4 servers" sounds rigorous and isn't.
  "Tens of servers" is the honest answer, and it's enough to decide.

## Habits that keep it honest

- **State assumptions before computing**, and write them where the
  interviewer (or your team) can see them. Wrong assumptions are easy to fix
  if they're visible.
- **Round aggressively.** 86,400 → 10<sup>5</sup>, 2.6M → 3M. Keep one
  significant figure, multiply in powers of ten.
- **Average, then peak.** Size for peak (2–10× the average, depending on
  how spiky traffic is) and storage for growth.
- **Separate reads from writes.** They usually differ by orders of magnitude
  and lead to different decisions: caching and replicas for reads,
  partitioning and queues for writes.
- **Watch for the multiplier you forgot**: replication factor (×3),
  indexes, metadata, backups, logs. Storage estimates are often low by
  several times.
- **Sanity-check against something known.** If your estimate says a chat
  app for a small company needs 500 servers, an assumption is wrong.

## Numbers age; ratios don't

Hardware keeps getting faster, so specific figures — requests per core,
cache-node throughput, SSD latency — drift over the years. The *ratios*
between them hold up much better: memory is roughly a thousand times faster
than an SSD read, which is far faster than a network round trip, which is
far faster than crossing an ocean. Designs are decided by those ratios.
When a precise number matters, measure it on your own hardware with your
own workload, and use the estimate only to decide what's worth measuring.
